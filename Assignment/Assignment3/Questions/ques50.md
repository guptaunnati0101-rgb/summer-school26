Over this assignment, I moved from single-sensor exercises to full end-to-end IoT systems — reading data from sensors like the DHT11, MQ-2, HC-SR04, and a PIR motion detector; processing that data with debouncing, rolling averages, and hysteresis logic; and acting on it through relays, servos, buzzers, and LCD displays. I also worked through the networking side: comparing MQTT and HTTP, understanding QoS guarantees, and seeing firsthand why unauthenticated public brokers and hardcoded credentials are such common real-world vulnerabilities rather than just textbook warnings.
The biggest learning shift for me was realizing that "getting the sensor to give a number" is the easy part — the real engineering is in the decision logic around that number: what threshold counts as dangerous smoke, how do you avoid false triggers from noisy ultrasonic echoes, how do you keep a keypad responsive during a 30-second countdown without blocking the whole program with delay(). Working through the water-level and greenhouse projects in particular taught me to think in terms of rolling averages and hysteresis bands rather than raw instantaneous readings — small design choices that separate a fragile demo from something that could survive real deployment conditions.
The security-focused questions were an unexpected highlight. Analyzing a vulnerable IoT snippet and having to name specific exploitable weaknesses — not just "it's insecure," but exactly why an unauthenticated public MQTT broker lets an attacker inject spoofed commands — made the abstract idea of "IoT security" concrete. I now think about every project, even class demos, in terms of: where do secrets live, is the transport encrypted, and does the device validate what it's told to do.
I can already see IoT skills applying directly to my field of study. Environmental and agricultural sensing (soil moisture, greenhouse control) connects to sustainability-focused problems I care about, and the security analysis work is directly transferable to any embedded or backend systems role, since the same "never trust unvalidated input" principle applies far beyond IoT.
For my next project, I'd like to build a campus energy-monitoring system: clamp-on current sensors (non-invasive, around classroom AC/lighting circuits) reporting to an ESP32 gateway over MQTT, aggregating usage per building block, and surfacing a dashboard that highlights which rooms are running HVAC or lights when unoccupied — cross-referenced with a PIR-based occupancy sensor, similar to the security-trigger project here. The interesting technical challenge would be doing this at scale, dozens of sensor nodes per building, while keeping the MQTT topic structure organized and the broker properly authenticated, learning directly from the Q49 exercise. Longer term, I'd want to add a simple anomaly-detection model on the aggregated data, extending the "rolling average" idea from the health-monitor project into a larger scale predictive pipeline that flags abnormal energy usage patterns automatically.
